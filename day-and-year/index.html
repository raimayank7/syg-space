Days and years on the planets

A day is one turn of a planet. A year is one trip round the Sun. Both differ wildly from world to world.

Two kinds of day

The rotation period is the time a planet takes to turn once compared with the distant stars. The length of day is the time from one noon to the next. They differ because a planet moves along its orbit while it spins, so it must turn a little more, or less, to bring the Sun back overhead. For Earth the two are close: 23.9 and 24 hours.

The shortest and longest days

Among the planets, Jupiter has the shortest day, 9.9 hours. Mercury has the longest, 175.9 Earth days.

Mercury: a day longer than its year

Mercury turns once in 1,407.6 hours, which is 58.6 Earth days, but its year is only 88 Earth days. Counted from noon to noon, one day on Mercury lasts 175.9 Earth days: about two of its own years.

Venus: backwards and slow

The minus sign beside Venus means that it turns the opposite way to Earth. One turn takes 243 Earth days, longer than its year of 224.7 days. On Venus the Sun would rise in the west.

The giants spin fast

Jupiter, the largest planet, turns once in just 9.9 hours, and Saturn in 10.7. Uranus (17.2 hours, also with a minus sign) and Neptune (16.1 hours) are not far behind.

Years grow with distance

The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its path and the slower it travels: Mercury moves at 47.4 km/s and Neptune at only 5.4 km/s. So Neptune takes 163.7 Earth years to go round once.
